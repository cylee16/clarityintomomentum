You Don’t Have a Discipline Problem

Why being hard on yourself keeps you stuck, and what helps instead · by Cylee

You know what you should do. You just don’t do it.

Or you do it for a while. Then a busy week hits, a day goes sideways, and you’re back where you started, saying the same things to yourself you always say.

You might describe yourself as fine. In a good place, but… On maintenance. Not in crisis, not really thriving. Stuck in a loop you can’t quite explain.

From my own life and from working with women who say exactly those words, I’ve come to believe this: what keeps you stuck usually isn’t a lack of motivation, information, or willpower. It’s the voice that shows up the moment you slip.

I remember waking up one morning already overwhelmed. The day had barely started. When I finally paid attention to what was running through my head, it was all negative. Every bit of it. What if this kid does this? What if that happens? A never-ending loop of all the ways my day was going to go wrong, and underneath it, the quiet belief that I couldn’t handle it.

The loop that looks like a discipline problem

It usually goes like this:

  1. You set an intention. Move more, go to bed earlier, stop eating after dinner, finally deal with the pile.
  2. Life happens, and you miss. One day, then two.
  3. The critic arrives. See? This is why. You always do this. What’s wrong with you?
  4. That feels awful, so you get away from it. You shut down, you scroll, you decide you’ll start fresh Monday. Or you quietly stop trying, because trying again means risking that feeling again.
  5. The miss becomes evidence. You tell yourself you’re just not a person who follows through. The loop tightens.

Most of us believe the critic is what keeps us in line. If I stopped being hard on myself, I’d never get anything done. That belief is worth checking against the evidence, because the evidence mostly points the other way.

What the research says

Self-criticism is built on threat. Psychologist Paul Gilbert, who developed compassion-focused therapy, describes self-criticism as the mind’s threat system turned inward. In that state you’re braced and defensive, which is useful in a crisis and poor for planning or trying something new. Brain-imaging work with his colleagues has found that self-criticism and self-reassurance engage noticeably different brain regions.

Shame tends to make us hide. June Tangney’s research separates two feelings. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.” In her work, guilt is more often linked to repair and trying again, and shame to hiding and withdrawal. Most inner critics speak fluent shame.

Self-compassion can increase effort. In one of four experiments by Breines and Chen (2012), students who failed a hard test and were guided to be understanding toward themselves spent more time studying for the next one than students in the comparison groups, including one given a self-esteem boost. In another, people reflecting on something they regretted were more motivated to make amends and not repeat it.

Harshness can turn one slip into a spiral. Researchers call it the disinhibition effect. People trying hard to restrict what they eat often eat more after breaking a rule. In a study by Adams and Leary (2007), the most restrictive eaters who were guided toward self-compassion after eating a doughnut felt less distressed and ate less afterward.

Forgiving yourself is linked to less procrastination. Wohl, Pychyl, and Bennett (2010) followed 119 first-year students through two midterms. Among those who had procrastinated on the first exam, the ones who forgave themselves for it procrastinated less before the second.

To be honest about the research: these are mostly small studies, often with college students. But they point the same way, and larger reviews link self-compassion to more healthy habits, like eating well, exercising, and sleeping, not fewer. Being kind to yourself after a miss doesn’t make you lazy. It makes trying again feel possible.

Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off the hook

This is where most women push back, and it’s a fair objection.

Self-compassion doesn’t mean lowering your standards. You keep the standard and drop the punishment.

Punishment asks: What’s wrong with me? Accountability asks: What happened, and what’s next?

The first question looks backward and has no useful answer. The second looks forward and almost always does.

Here’s a question that helps me when the critic is loud: How would the kindest, wisest presence I can imagine speak to me about this? For me, that’s God — grace I can’t earn and don’t have to. For you it might be something else. Either way, that voice almost never sounds like the critic. The critic says earn your way back. Grace, in any form, says you don’t have to punish yourself to meet the standard — you just have to keep it, and begin again.

Compassion-focused therapy uses this same move on purpose: picture a compassionate other, because a kinder voice is easier to borrow before it’s easy to find in yourself.

One honest note: if you’ve been hard on yourself for a long time, kindness can feel strange at first, even uncomfortable. Gilbert’s work notes this is common for highly self-critical people. It’s not a sign you’re doing it wrong. It’s a sign it’s new.

What we actually do about it

This is less about affirmations and more about small, practical moves you can make on a regular Tuesday.

1. Notice it and name it. You don’t have to win an argument with the critic, or force a positive thought in its place. You just have to see it. There’s that voice again. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy calls this “defusion”: stepping back far enough to see a thought as a thought, not the truth. That bit of distance is often enough to interrupt the spiral.

These days, when I hear it, I say something like: Oh look, my mind is pointing out the negative again. I’m choosing the positive today. I don’t need that voice right now. It will only bring my energy down.

2. Ask a better question. Swap “Why can’t I just…” for “What got in the way, and what’s one small thing I could try?” Motivational interviewing research suggests that pushing people tends to make them argue for staying the same. Drawing out their own reasons works better. That’s true when the pushing comes from inside your own head, too.
3. Set a floor for the hard days. All-or-nothing is the critic’s favorite game. Decide ahead of time what the smallest version of your habit is: ten minutes, one vegetable, lights out half an hour earlier. On a bad day, the floor counts. That’s how one rough Wednesday stops costing you the whole week.

I had a workout program I loved on my good days. On the hard days, I’d open it, feel overwhelmed, and not work out at all. Now my floor is simple: Can I walk for 15 minutes?

4. Give yourself permission to rest. If rest has to be earned, you’ll never feel you’ve earned enough. Rest isn’t the reward for finishing the list. It’s part of what makes the list possible. Rest was built into creation in the very first week, before anyone had done a thing to earn it.
5. Measure what moved, not only what didn’t. The critic keeps a very selective scorecard. In my programs we use a Whole-Life Assessment across nine areas of life, revisited at key checkpoints along the way, so you can see where things are actually shifting. Women almost always already know where their leverage point is. What they’re usually missing is proof that it’s working.

What changes when the critic loosens its grip

When women tell me what they actually want, it’s rarely a number, though sometimes it is. Most want to trust themselves to keep the promises they make. They want to feel proud of themselves, not thinner or busier. They want to feel lighter, calmer, less braced. They want to stop beating themselves up when a day doesn’t go as planned. They want to have energy and feel good.

When the critic stops running the show, follow-through gets easier. You’re not suddenly trying harder. Trying again has just stopped costing so much.

Behavior scientist BJ Fogg puts it simply in Tiny Habits: “People change best by feeling good, not by feeling bad.” I’d add one thing. It isn’t about feeling good about yourself all the time. The real test is how you talk to yourself when things fall apart.

A note on what coaching is and isn’t: if your inner critic is tangled up with depression, trauma, or thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out to a therapist or your doctor. Coaching is forward-looking work, and it pairs well with that support. It doesn’t replace it.

If you read this and recognized yourself, you’re not broken and you’re not lazy. You’ve been running a system that was never going to work.
